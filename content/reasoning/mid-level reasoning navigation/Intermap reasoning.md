  	- I don't really have anything important to say about search anymore, but I do about *explore* - "explore" is when you don't know what you're looking for - you can't find what you need by typing in keywords related to the question, or asking a chatbot, because you don't know what there is to find out - you don't know what useful to know, and out of that, what is known (by at least someone), and out of that, what is on the internet and public. This also applied to looking for images etc where it isn't about learning a fact, it's just enjoying looking at the thing directly.
  	- The main method of exploring is going through list of links (to content) or content itself. Multiple links/pieces of  information are shown a page at once - usually in a list or grid. ...

The most useful way of "exploring" on Wikipedia at the moment is the Content Portals. Here, the content on (English) Wikipedia is divided into "General reference", "Culture and the arts", "Geography and places", etc. This is still pretty random/illogical and has a lot of room for improvement.

There have been big improvements in "search", as in finding a specific piece of information you know you want, in the last ~12 years since I started thinking about this. There ... .
  	- The main way "explore" has improved is the advent of *feeds* - these perform the function of explore to some extent because they tell you about things you didn't know exist. But you still can't really use them to discover completely new ideas quickly. They don't give you a high-level overview of what exists, and let you consciously cross off categories of things you're not interested in, or are on your list of areas to explore. Knowing that you've gone through all the categories you're *not* interested in, and that the ones left are *all of* the things to be interested in, is important.
  	- There has been no noticeable improvement in the structure of content/navigation in the "explore" aspect of the internet in that 12 years. My framework from as early as 2014 is still relevant.

  	- My main idea (as of 2026/05) for organizing knowledge on the internet, for *exploring*, is using the multifaceted system I use on my PKM, with argument stage as the primary facet, and many other facets as secondary facets.
  	- The main type of knowledge people would be *exploring* would be general knowledge, and all the reasoning involved with it, as opposed to planning reasoning. What I call "knowledge stage reasoning" and "planning stage reasoning" on my PKM respectively.
  	- So the main argument stages for this would be Data stage reasoning, Correlation stage reasoning, and Model stage reasoning (I'm revising my system in ~2026/05/13 from the "Data stage", "Sample Correlation stage", "Populational Correlation stage", and "Global Model stage" that are still in my notes at the moment).
  	- Those 3 stages are going to be subdivided. I haven't really figured out the next level of subdivisions yet.
  	- For "decorative content", I haven't had any really original ideas. At the moment, the taxonomy is just going to be based on popular categories that correspond to interests like "vehicles", "travel", "food". The difference with my system is I'll define/refine them so they don't overlap. Being clear about overlap vs non-overlap of categories is really important for browsing efficiently at this level. It basically stops you from having to click into a category then see lots of the stuff you thought you "crossed off" by seeing previously then checking "not interested".
  	- Secondary facets would include:
  		- Possible opinion vs "task-question" - information is divided into questions and opinions, instead of being seen as just consisting of opinions
  		- Author - who's uploaded the fact/image/etc/set of facts/iamges/etc
  		- Author's confidence level ("probability interval" on my PKM) (for an opinion)
  		- Another person's confidence level (for an opinion)
  		- A weighted sum of everyone's confidence level in an opinion (for everyone who's "rated" it by adding their own metadata)
  		- Informal vs formal writing style.
  		- Date added
  		- Date the author formed the opinion (could be much earlier than the date published)
  		- Question-that-it's-an-answer-to (for opinions) - this filter would return all opinions to a given (exact) topic/question, on the whole internet. This is a really fine-grained category though - you'd only be browsing through a list of these when you're already in a pretty small area - e.g. sub-sub-sub-sub-field of something like physics or "2026 world news" might have few enough specific questions (e.g. <20) that you could go through a list of them on a page.

  	- The main advantage of this over feeds is "wide/exhaustive scope" - it lets you find *everything* you're interested in, and lets you *know* when you have and haven't found everything you're interested in.
  	- Feeds in comparison are very efficient in terms of doing a decent job of finding things for people with minimal effort. They're good for people who don't want to put much effort into exploring they're interests. For this reason, they make a lot of sense from a purely commercial point of view.

Maybe as many as half of all people are basically hardwired to go about life in a mindless way. But I think there are a large minority of people who could end up exploring their interests in a more thoughtful way like this.
